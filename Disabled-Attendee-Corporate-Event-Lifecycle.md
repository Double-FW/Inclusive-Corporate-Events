# Accessibility in the Corporate Event Lifecycle

**Designing an event experience disabled people can choose, access and participate in**

## Purpose

This guide maps a corporate event from the perspective of a disabled attendee, from first hearing about it to leaving feedback afterwards. It is for event owners, communications teams, venue and production partners, presenters, platform providers and anyone using AI to plan or deliver an event.

It covers in-person, remote and hybrid events. The goal is not simply to make a venue or livestream technically accessible. An attendee should be able to understand what the event offers, decide whether it will work for them, register without unnecessary disclosure, prepare for the experience, participate in the sessions and social parts they choose, and give feedback in a way that is accessible and safe.

Accessibility is part of event quality and delivery. It should be planned, budgeted, tested and owned from the start. Individual adjustments remain important, but they should sit alongside proactive design rather than carry the whole responsibility for access.

## Principles

- **Choice and autonomy:** provide accurate information and meaningful options so people can decide how to participate.
- **Access by design:** build access into the venue, digital experience, content, communications and operating plan.
- **No single way to participate:** offer equivalent ways to receive information, communicate, move through the event and take part.
- **Respect and privacy:** ask only for information needed to arrange access; explain who will see it and how it will be used.
- **Nothing about us without us:** involve disabled people, including paid lived-experience expertise where appropriate, in planning and evaluation.
- **Access is operational:** assign named owners, budgets, suppliers, checks and contingency plans.
- **Continuous improvement:** use feedback and observed friction to improve the next event, while protecting individual data.

## Lifecycle overview

1. Awareness, invitation and event information
2. Registration, ticketing and access requests
3. Preparation and pre-event information
4. Arrival, orientation and participation
5. Presentations, networking and event operations
6. Departure, feedback and organisational learning

The stages overlap. A late venue change, inaccessible platform update or revised agenda can affect earlier decisions, so event teams should keep information current and tell attendees when material changes.

## Stage 1: Awareness, invitation and event information

### The attendee experience

The attendee first encounters the event through an email, website, invitation, social post, colleague or booking platform. They need to understand what the event is, who it is for, what will happen, how to join, what it costs, and whether the format is likely to work for them. They may be deciding whether to disclose a need, ask a question, request an adjustment or decline because the information is too uncertain.

### Organiser actions

- Publish event information in accessible HTML where possible. Use clear headings, descriptive links, plain language, accessible images and captions or transcripts for promotional video.
- Ensure invitations and booking journeys work with keyboard navigation, screen readers, magnification, voice control and mobile devices. Check that colour, layout and time limits do not create avoidable barriers.
- State whether the event is in-person, remote or hybrid; whether remote participation is genuinely interactive; and which parts can be accessed live or later.
- Describe the venue and access provision in concrete terms: step-free routes, accessible toilets, seating choices, quiet or low-stimulation space, lighting, likely noise, catering arrangements, parking and public transport. Do not rely on a general claim that a venue is “accessible.”
- Explain available communication access, such as live captions, sign language interpretation, hearing loop or assistive listening, transcripts, audio description, speech-to-text support and accessible presentation materials. Be clear about what is confirmed and what is still being arranged.
- Give a named contact and more than one way to reach them. Explain response times and how to request information in another format.
- Avoid implying that disabled attendees must justify their attendance or disclose a diagnosis to receive basic access information.

### Barriers to identify

Images of event details without text equivalents; inaccessible registration links; vague venue claims; missing travel or sensory information; video without captions; invitation-only information that cannot be forwarded accessibly; unclear costs for a support worker; and promotion that presents remote access as equivalent when it is only a passive stream.

### Intended outcome

The attendee can make an informed choice and knows how to ask questions or request access without unnecessary effort or disclosure.

## Stage 2: Registration, ticketing and access requests

### The attendee experience

The attendee registers, chooses a participation mode and may request adjustments for themselves or a presenter role. They may need to book a companion or support worker, specify seating or communication access, explain dietary needs, or ask for an alternative to a standard ticketing process. They need confidence that a request will be received, handled discreetly and fulfilled.

### Organiser actions

- Make the full ticketing journey accessible, including account creation, payment, error messages, confirmation and cancellation. Provide a human-assisted registration route with equivalent terms.
- Ask only for information needed to deliver the event or a requested adjustment. Separate access information from general marketing and attendee lists; restrict access to staff who need it.
- Offer an open text field and useful, non-exhaustive options. Do not force people to choose a diagnosis or disclose personal medical details.
- Allow attendees to register a companion or support worker without an additional charge where applicable, and explain the process clearly.
- Confirm each request, say who owns it, identify any unresolved items and give a date for follow-up. Never imply that a request is guaranteed until it has been confirmed.
- Provide a way to update a request and explain how late requests will be handled. Keep a private operational record of the adjustment, not unnecessary diagnostic information.
- Protect access-request information from being shared with presenters, sponsors or other attendees unless the attendee has agreed and sharing is necessary.

### Remote and hybrid registration

Explain the platform, device or browser requirements, login process, caption and interpretation options, how to ask questions, how to request technical support, and whether the attendee can join by phone. Offer a way to test the platform and provide dial-in or low-bandwidth options where feasible. Do not assume remote participation removes all access barriers.

### Intended outcome

The attendee can complete registration, choose how to participate, request access and receive a clear confirmation without having to repeatedly explain the same need.

## Stage 3: Preparation and pre-event information

### The attendee experience

Before the event, the attendee plans travel, work, energy, medication, communication and technology. Predictability can determine whether attendance is possible. They may want to know the room layout, likely transitions, session length, audience participation style, food arrangements, sensory conditions or how to leave and re-enter.

### Organiser actions

Send an accessible information pack early enough to be useful and update it when details change. Include:

- final agenda with start and finish times, breaks, session formats and expected transitions
- venue address, accessible entrance, travel options, parking and arrival instructions
- maps in accessible formats, with a plain-language route description and key landmarks
- room layouts, accessible seating options, quiet space, toilets, prayer or rest space and help points
- what to expect at check-in, security, networking, meals and any social activity
- contact details for the event access lead and on-the-day support
- confirmed interpretation, captions, assistive listening and other communication arrangements
- remote joining instructions, platform accessibility notes, tech support and a joining test
- accessible pre-reading and slides when available, with a clear explanation if materials will be shared later
- emergency and evacuation information, including how to ask for assistance and what arrangements have been made

Keep the pack usable on a phone and available as accessible web content or a tagged document. Avoid relying on a map image or a QR code as the only route to essential information.

### Presenter preparation

Brief presenters, chairs, hosts, moderators and sponsors before the event. The brief should cover:

- the event’s access commitments and who to contact if something fails
- microphone use, speaking pace, introductions and repeating audience questions
- describing meaningful visual content and avoiding “as you can see” without explanation
- using accessible slides, sufficient contrast, readable type, clear structure and restrained animation
- sharing materials in advance where possible and providing accessible formats
- keeping captions and interpreters visible and not speaking over them
- how to manage questions, online chat and audience participation inclusively
- not asking attendees to disclose disability or explain access arrangements publicly
- respecting breaks, time boundaries and different ways of participating

Presenters who have access requirements should be contacted privately and early. Confirm transport, step-free stage access, lectern and microphone options, green room access, communication support, rehearsal arrangements, captioning or interpretation, and any changes to the running order. Do not assume that a presenter’s needs are the same as an attendee’s or ask them to arrange their own access with every supplier.

### Intended outcome

Attendees and presenters can prepare with reliable information, and the event team can resolve access needs before they become on-the-day emergencies.

## Stage 4: Arrival, orientation and participation

### In-person attendee experience

The attendee travels to the venue, finds the correct entrance, reaches registration and navigates to the spaces they need. Arrival is often the first test of whether published access information was accurate. An unclear entrance, queue, badge process or route can make the person dependent on help before the event has begun.

### Organiser actions

- Check the route from transport drop-off and accessible parking to the entrance, registration, event rooms, toilets and quiet space. Keep routes clear and step-free where promised.
- Provide visible, readable, high-contrast signage with consistent wording, symbols and arrows. Place signs where a decision is needed, not only at the destination. Do not use colour alone to distinguish routes or rooms.
- Provide staffed orientation and clear ways to request help without making assistance compulsory. Brief staff on respectful language, guide and assistance dogs, mobility aids, queue options and how to ask before helping.
- Offer seating at registration, a way to avoid or reduce queues, and alternatives to name badges or lanyards where these create barriers.
- Check lighting, acoustics, temperature, crowding and seating. Make accessible seating integrated into the audience rather than isolated, and keep wheelchair spaces flexible for companions.
- Tell attendees where captions, interpreters, hearing support, accessible toilets, quiet areas, lifts and exits are located. Confirm equipment is working before doors open.
- Ensure the stage and presenter route are accessible. Provide a contingency if a lift, hearing system, caption feed or other key provision fails.

### Remote attendee experience

The attendee joins from a computer, phone or other device and must be able to find the session, understand who is speaking, follow content, use captions or interpretation, ask questions and get help. They should not have to rely on a separate, inaccessible channel to participate.

### Organiser actions

- Open the online room early, provide a clear welcome and explain how to use captions, interpretation, chat, reactions, questions and support.
- Use an accessible platform and test the actual event configuration, including keyboard access, screen-reader announcements, captions, interpreter view, shared content, breakout rooms and recording controls.
- Provide a co-host or technical support contact who can respond privately and promptly. Ensure moderation does not remove access-related questions or contributions.
- Make the online experience part of the event plan: remote attendees need opportunities to contribute, not only to watch.
- Provide equivalent breaks, session materials and networking options where possible. State any limits honestly.

### Intended outcome

Attendees can orient themselves, access essential spaces or digital controls, and begin participating with independence, dignity and a reliable route to help.

## Stage 5: Presentations, networking and event operations

### The attendee experience

The attendee listens, watches, reads, asks questions, meets people, eats, rests and moves between sessions. Different formats may create different barriers: fast speech, overlapping conversation, inaccessible slides, standing-only networking, flashing visuals, unclear turn-taking, poor sound, hidden online questions or insufficient breaks.

### Organiser and presenter actions

- Start and end sessions on time and keep planned breaks. Announce changes and explain where the next activity takes place.
- Use microphones for every speaker and audience question. Repeat questions before answering; do not rely on a handheld microphone being passed quickly around the room.
- Ensure live captions and interpretation are visible, accurate enough to follow and supported by appropriate sound, lighting and sightlines. Have a named person monitor delivery and a contingency plan.
- Make presentations accessible: structured slides, readable text, contrast, meaningful alt text where useful, described charts and images, no information conveyed by colour alone, and no unnecessary flashing or motion.
- With permission from the presenter, provide accessible copies of materials in advance or promptly afterwards. Avoid inaccessible handouts, image-only PDFs and last-minute QR-only access.
- Make participation options explicit: speak, use chat, submit questions privately, use a support person, or observe without being called on. Do not make eye contact, standing, rapid response or public disclosure a condition of participation.
- Design networking and meals with varied ways to take part, accessible routes and seating, clear food labels, a quieter option and staff who can answer questions discreetly.
- Monitor the full event, including transitions, exhibition areas and social activities, for access barriers. Access does not stop when the main session ends.

### Hybrid parity

Plan room microphones, cameras, screens, chat moderation and speaker interaction so remote attendees can hear and be heard. Avoid side conversations that are inaudible online. Make a named host responsible for bringing remote questions into the room and room questions to remote participants. If a feature cannot be made equivalent, tell attendees before they choose a ticket.

### If access fails

The event lead should acknowledge the issue, tell affected attendees what is being done and offer a practical alternative. Do not ask an attendee to troubleshoot alone or disclose more personal information in public. Record the failure, impact, owner and resolution for follow-up.

### Intended outcome

Attendees can engage with the content and each other in ways that suit them, and access arrangements remain operational throughout the event.

## Stage 6: Departure, feedback and organisational learning

### The attendee experience

The attendee leaves the venue or online room, travels home and may need follow-up materials or a way to report what worked and what did not. Departure can create barriers when transport is crowded, routes are unclear, the event overruns, accessible transport has not been considered or online attendees are abruptly disconnected.

### Organiser actions

- Give clear closing and departure information, including accessible routes, transport updates, accessible parking and where to get help.
- End at the advertised time where possible. If the schedule changes, explain the change and its practical implications.
- Send accessible slides, recordings, transcripts, captions and follow-up information promptly. Provide a contact for inaccessible or missing materials.
- Offer feedback through accessible formats and channels, including a short form, email or private conversation. Make feedback optional and explain how it will be used.
- Ask about the experience and barriers without requiring diagnosis or medical detail. Include questions about communications, registration, venue or platform, content, presenters, participation, adjustments and overall autonomy.
- Make a clear route for complaints or urgent concerns and explain confidentiality and response times.
- Review feedback with disabled people and relevant suppliers. Track actions, owners and dates; tell attendees what changed when appropriate.

### Intended outcome

Attendees can leave safely, access event outputs and influence future events without being burdened with responsibility for fixing the system.

## Cross-cutting governance and ownership

Every event should have a named accountable event owner and a named access lead with authority to coordinate decisions. The access lead may be a dedicated specialist or an explicitly assigned role within an embedded team. Responsibility should be shared across communications, ticketing, venue, production, catering, security, presenters and digital platform suppliers; it should not be transferred to disabled attendees.

At minimum, the event plan should record:

- access requirements and expected outcomes for each lifecycle stage
- named owners, suppliers, due dates and budget
- attendee requests and their status, stored with appropriate privacy controls
- confirmed provision, unresolved risks and contingency arrangements
- accessibility checks of communications, ticketing, venue, platform and materials
- incident and feedback records, with actions tracked through completion

Accessibility requirements should be included in supplier briefs and contracts, with evidence, testing, issue reporting and remediation expectations. A supplier’s general assurance should not substitute for checking the actual event experience.

## AI agent guidance for event planning and delivery

AI systems may help draft communications, review plans, identify risks, answer routine questions or summarise feedback. They must support accountable human delivery and must not make access decisions that deny participation or treat an attendee’s disability as a problem to manage.

### Required behaviour

For every event task or decision, an AI agent should:

1. Identify the lifecycle stage, participation mode and responsible human owner.
2. Check whether the task creates barriers or assumes a single way to communicate, move, perceive, process or participate.
3. Check whether access information is accurate, current, complete and consistent across channels.
4. Protect personal access information and use only what is needed for the stated purpose.
5. Explain the barrier, who may be affected, evidence and practical consequence.
6. Recommend a remedy, owner and date; escalate unresolved or high-impact issues before the event proceeds.
7. Record the decision and verify that the remedy was delivered.

### Mandatory escalation triggers

Escalate to the event owner and access lead when:

- a requested adjustment is rejected, unresolved or cannot be confirmed
- published access information is inaccurate or materially incomplete
- ticketing, venue, platform, content or communication access has not been checked
- a supplier cannot provide evidence or a workable contingency
- access arrangements fail during the event
- disability or access-request information is exposed or used beyond its stated purpose
- an event design excludes people from a session, networking, catering or social activity
- a presenter or attendee reports discrimination, stigma or exclusion
- feedback shows a repeated barrier or a pattern of unequal experience

### Prohibited behaviours

An AI agent must not:

- infer disability or access needs from a person’s name, appearance, behaviour or browsing data
- require a diagnosis as a condition of basic access information or an adjustment request
- disclose an attendee’s access request to presenters, sponsors or other attendees without a justified need and appropriate permission
- claim that a venue, platform or event is accessible without evidence
- promise an adjustment, interpretation, captioning or other service that has not been confirmed by its owner
- treat a livestream as equivalent participation without checking interaction and access
- minimise or dismiss feedback because a barrier affected only one person
- make a final decision to refuse participation or an adjustment

### Escalation brief template

```text
EVENT ACCESSIBILITY BRIEF
Date:
Event and lifecycle stage:
To: Event owner / access lead
From:

ISSUE
What is the barrier or risk?

IMPACT

EVIDENCE

RECOMMENDED ACTION

CONTINGENCY
What will happen if the issue is not resolved by the deadline?

STATUS AND REVIEW
Decision, date, outcome and next review:
```

## Measuring and improving the event

Measures should support learning, not surveillance. Useful indicators include registration completion and abandonment; access-request response and fulfilment; availability and reliability of captions or interpretation; accessibility defects identified and resolved; attendee-reported ability to participate; access-related incidents; and completion of improvement actions. Collect only the data needed, report trends in aggregate where possible and restrict access to identifiable information.

A high satisfaction score does not prove that an event was accessible to everyone. Review reports of barriers, people who could not attend or complete registration, and differences between in-person, remote and hybrid experiences. Absence of complaints is not evidence of absence of barriers.
